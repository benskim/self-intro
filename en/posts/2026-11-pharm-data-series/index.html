How far can public data ease pharmacies’ problems?

Price cuts and prescription demand, seen through the reimbursement list and drug usage statistics

Series overview
Medicine data
Public medicine data series
Overview of a series that sorts pharmacy pain points by the path a drug takes through a pharmacy (procuring, holding stock, dispensing, earning) and analyses the parts that public medicine data can address.
Author

Ben Kim

Published

November 27, 2026

This series asks how far public medicine data can ease the problems pharmacies face. The perspective is that of those who could help pharmacies with ordering, stock and return information (distributors, pharmacy support services). Each page starts from what makes pharmacy work hard and ends with what public information can tell in advance. The full Korean version of this overview is here.

Why these questions

Stage Question What leaks Felt rank Public data coverage
A. Procuring Can the drugs needed be obtained on time, in the amount needed? Time, dispensing opportunities 1 Partly. Demand (prescriptions) and the number of substitutes; not shortages, allocation or stock
B. Holding stock Does stock already bought keep its value? Money 2 (worsening fastest) Most. What is cut, when and by how much
C. Dispensing Is dispensing manageable? Time, consumables, disputes 3 Weakly. Volume per prescription
D. Earning Are sales and margins holding? Sales 4 None. No pharmacy- or area-level data

Procuring is felt most today; holding stock is a close second and is expected to worsen fastest as listed drugs are re-evaluated from 2027. The stages are linked: when drugs are hard to get, pharmacies stock up, and stock loses value when prices are cut.

What public data can and cannot do. They can tell in advance what will be cut, when and by how much; single out ingredients whose demand swings; and show how far announcements can be trusted. They cannot compute a pharmacy’s losses (that needs its stock records), forecast shortages (no supply or stock data), or change compensation and return terms. The pain points and cases rest mainly on trade press and pharmacist surveys, which may lean towards reported harm.

Does combining the two datasets help?

Adding reimbursement-list price information to demand forecasts did not show an improvement. With the price changes visible in the list at forecast time, unit-price forecast error was 0.0186 vs 0.0217, about 15% lower, but p = 0.252 could not be told apart from chance, nor from forecasts with the timing deliberately shifted. For prescription volume, the test could not even detect a deliberately injected 20% effect. The median pass-through from list price change to claimed unit price was only 0.55. The series therefore uses the reimbursement list for price and suspension alerts and the usage statistics for demand. The table is in the technical note.

Ground rules

  • A future date in the remarks is an announcement, not a change. Pages say “a cut was announced” or “realization rate 99.96%”, never “the price was cut”.
  • Price changes and prescription volume are compared only as before-and-after changes and associations, not as causes.
  • Demand forecasts are national and per ingredient, not order quantities for a pharmacy or product.
  • No savings or incentive amounts are computed.
  • The data contain no shortages, product-level sales, pharmacy purchases or stock, wholesale distribution, or planned delisting dates.
